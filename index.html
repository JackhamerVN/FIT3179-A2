TRAVEL, TRANSPORT & TOURISM JUL 2014 – JUN 2026

Domestic air travel
in Australia.

Back to the old total, but not to the old network.

In 2025–26 domestic flights carried 60.75 million passenger trips, almost exactly as many as before the pandemic. This page looks behind that total: how it compares with a growing population, where people fly, which routes gained and lost, and how airlines responded.

THE RECOVERY

Back to the old total.
In a bigger country.

Passenger trips collapsed in 2020 and took until 2025–26 to come within 0.4% of their 2018–19 level. By then, Australia had 2.64 million more residents.

02

THE NETWORK

A big country.
A concentrated network.

Most passengers fly a handful of interstate routes between the largest cities. The ten busiest routes carry half of all trips.

WINNERS & LOSERS

Some routes boomed.
Others never came back.

Comparing the same 57 routes in 2018–19 and 2025–26, 30 carried more passengers and 27 carried fewer.

04

STATES & AIRPORTS

Growth in the west.
Weight in the east.

On the same 57 routes, Western Australia’s airport passengers grew by 19.5% while the ACT’s fell by 18.5%. A map of change, however, gives the most ink to the largest states.

05

FULLER PLANES

Fewer seats.
Fuller planes.

Airlines flew 2.9% fewer seats in 2025–26 than in 2018–19 while carrying almost as many passengers. The share of seats filled rose from 78.7% to 80.7%.

THE TAKEAWAY

The total came back.
The network did not.

Domestic flights carried almost as many passenger trips in 2025–26 as in 2018–19, but Australia now has 2.6 million more residents, so air travel has not kept pace with the population. Growth went to Perth’s mining towns and to holiday destinations; short routes in the south-east and Melbourne–Sydney, the busiest of all, carry fewer people. Airlines flew fewer seats, and planes are fuller than before the pandemic.

Natural Earth: state boundaries.

Latest disclosed routes represent 53,982,647 trips, or 88.9% of the national total. National and city-pair statistics include visitors taking domestic trips and domestic stages of Australian international flights. International stages are excluded. Chart 03 compares the growth of this domestic activity with the growth of the resident population; it does not measure how often residents themselves fly. Chart 13 uses BITRE’s separate airport series, whose coverage differs: domestic legs of international services are generally excluded, except international flight numbers operating wholly between domestic airports since July 2011.
